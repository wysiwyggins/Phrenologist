> With this work and a Phrenological Head, Parents, and those having the Guardianship of Children and Youth, will be enabled to discover the capabilities and propensities of those under their charge, and direct their Education accordingly. (Taylor 1841)

Phrenology was a natural progression in the enlightenment project. Philosophy of mind came to understand that the brain operates in some sort of mechanical way, and that mind states and mental states were connected, either causally (brain states cause mental states) or that both were the phenomena of some kind of monadic primary mover. Today we generally accept that the condition of your brain gives rise to mental states, and that is a causal relationship. Where we still waffle however is whether this is a mechanical process is entirely connected to the external world with no opportunity for some kind of internal intervention, which would seem to contradict the phenomenological experience of initiating actions and making decisions. Phrenology was a sort of proto-neuroanatomy from a time when the brain could only be considered as a kind of muscle and that it must have regions that would buldge or atrophy in connection with the different mechanistic duties of perception, thought and action. In trying to prove the material nature of the brain and its connection to mental states, however, this undercooked pseudoscience leapt into practical application, and affected lives. Phrenology was a contested but popular field of study, with institutes that granted degrees. 19th century psychiatry and psychology were deeply enmeshed in phrenological ideas. As actual locations for things like speech activity were found in the brain, phrenology was only undermined by the location of these areas, and it was supersceded by neuropsychology, which had more effective research methods but an order of magnitude worse harms as far as how it was practically applied (I'm thinking here of forced lobotomies and electroshock therapy, etc.)

In the chiromancer example, there's no attempt to say that the lines on a palm *cause* fate, or *cause* character traits. The idea is that they are a sort of fingerprint that one can use to identify character, and having a deeper knowledge of that character, fate can be predicted to a certain extent. Phrenology is also this mantic reader mentality hitchhiking into scientific inquiry in that it seeks to *identify* character, but it slips into a causal relationship. Fate has dissapeared from the conversation since this is incompatible with modernity. The conversation swings entirely into the modern mode of control. Identifying temperaments and abilities means the ability to select servents, plan educations and assign labors.

In both of these sketches, I think I am wondering what it would be like if I had the time and skill to actually build out these absurdly complex systems of identification and play with them, since the game world in a sense *is* fated, if it is also unpredictable. It only has a few interventions- the inputs that affect RNG (the computer clock for instance) and the actions of the player. If you had a character generation system that determined the "actions" of the player character and eliminated or restricted the agency of the player, and simplified the RNG, the relationship between character and fate would be simply demonstrated as a thought experiment. The only thing you would stand to learn, however is what that *feels* like from a player perspective, after an immense feat of programming and game design.

Phrenology is interesting because it's so easy to dunk on it now, but people spent their lives fruitlessly studying it and, like palmistry, it was complex enough that you could see anything in it, and justify any preconceived notion in it. It had a few effects that I would consider vaguely positive— it was a new sort of middleground between free will and determinism (people were said to have different faculties but no one began life as inherently evil.) A working class person with desirable head lumps could be used to question the privelage of an aristocrat with an embarassingly shaped skull. It was of course used to argue for racist and sexist hierarchies, as you can no doubt imagine.
